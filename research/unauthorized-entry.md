# Entry without inspection (unauthorized entry): sourced reference for "Immigrant"

Research date: 2026-11-08. Scope: aggregated statistics, legal process, policy history, harms and outcomes only. No routes, locations, methods, smuggler contacts or enforcement-evasion detail, by design. This is not legal advice. Treat people who cross without inspection as a varied group (workers, families, asylum seekers, trafficking and crime victims, long-settled residents), not a stereotype.

Label key:
- CONFIRMED = a primary source (agency file, court or Federal Register text, agency PDF) was opened and read this session, or computed by me from such a file.
- LIKELY = secondary source (news, think tank summary, search snippet) or my background knowledge of a primary source I did not re-open. Check before shipping.
- UNVERIFIED = could not verify, or sources conflict. Do not show as fact.

Method note: CBP public CSVs were downloaded and summed by me (CONFIRMED arithmetic). Where I write "derived", the number is my sum or ratio of a primary file.

## 1. VOLUME

### 1.1 Terms the game must get right (CONFIRMED, CBP page text)
- "Encounters" = USBP (Border Patrol, between ports) Title 8 apprehensions + OFO (ports of entry) Title 8 inadmissibles + Title 42 expulsions (Title 42 expulsions ran 2020-03-21 to 2023-05-11). Source: https://www.cbp.gov/newsroom/stats/nationwide-encounters-fy2025
- OFO "inadmissibles" include people who present at a port seeking protection, people refused entry with visas at airports, and withdrawals of applications for admission. They are NOT all unauthorized crossers. This matters most for India: most Indian OFO counts nationwide are at non-land ports (airports), see 1.4.
- One person can be encountered more than once, so encounters are not unique people (CBP statistical caveat).
- From FY2024-25 CBP pages split Border Patrol apprehensions into "At Entry" (crossed, not yet at destination) and "At Large" (already settled or overstayed). Same source URL.

### 1.6 Unauthorized population and how people entered
- Pew, Aug 2025 (CONFIRMED, read via https://www.pewresearch.org/race-and-ethnicity/2025/08/21/u-s-unauthorized-immigrant-population-reached-a-record-14-million-in-2023/markdown): record 14 million in 2023; +3.5 million 2021 to 2023; Mexico about 4.3 million (about 30%, smallest Mexican share on record; Mexicans were a majority through 2016); India 680,000 (no significant change 2021 to 2023); Nigeria not reported by Pew. More than 12 million "entered illegally or overstayed"; about 2 million entered legally and were paroled or released. 4.2 million unauthorized adults had been here under 5 years in 2023 (1.8 million in 2021). Pew says the population likely stayed above 2023 levels in mid-2025 and that survey-based 2025 figures are preliminary.
- Pew does not split EWI vs overstay in this report. No recent Pew or DHS stock split was retrieved (GAP). Historic anchors: CMS found overstays outnumbered border crossers in the annual flow from FY2010 to FY2017 (about 62% of the newly undocumented in 2016-17 were overstays, 38% crossers) and overstays were about 42% of the undocumented stock in 2014 (LIKELY, https://www.ctpublic.org/2019-01-17/for-7th-consecutive-year-visa-overstays-exceeded-illegal-border-crossings ; https://www.immigrationresearch.org/node/2694). Pew 2006 had "as much as 45%" overstays (LIKELY, https://www.pewresearch.org/race-and-ethnicity/2006/05/22/modes-of-entry-for-the-unauthorized-migrant-population/). These pre-date the 2021-2024 surge, which was mostly border crossings, so the stock share since 2021 has shifted toward EWI/parole; no number verified.
- DHS Entry/Exit overstay reports cover air and sea only, exclude Canada and Mexico land arrivals, and give overstay rates by nationality; FY2024 national suspected in-country overstay rate 0.92% (427,204) per `country-nigeria-addons.md` 2.1 (CONFIRMED there).
- CMS 2023 provisional: undocumented population 11.7 million in July 2023 (LIKELY, headline only; CMS and Pew differ by method).

## 2. WHAT HAPPENS AFTER APPREHENSION OR PRESENTING AT THE BORDER

### 2.1 The process (CONFIRMED where the CRS source is cited; https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R48078.html)
1. Custody and processing by CBP. Possible tracks: (a) expedited removal under INA 235(b)(1); (b) Notice to Appear (NTA) and regular removal proceedings before an immigration judge (INA 240); (c) other dispositions (voluntary return, reinstatement of a prior order for people who re-enter after removal).
2. Expedited removal: an officer can order removal without a hearing. People who say they fear return or want asylum get a credible fear interview (CFI) with a USCIS asylum officer. By statute they "shall be detained" pending screening; parole is only case by case. A negative finding can be reviewed by an immigration judge, "to the maximum extent practicable within 24 hours, but in no case later than 7 days"; if the judge agrees, no appeal. A positive finding normally leads to regular removal proceedings (where the asylum claim is heard in full).
3. Expedited removal scope: historically border-area, within 14 days and 100 miles for people not at a port; in January 2025 DHS extended it to anyone inside the US for under 2 years. The D.C. Circuit (Make the Road New York v. Mullin, No. 25-5320) reversed the district court's block in June 2026 (Judge Walker; Wilkins dissenting) (LIKELY, https://media.cadc.uscourts.gov/opinions/docs/2026/06/25-5320-2179963.pdf ; https://www.courthousenews.com/dc-circuit-restores-trumps-expedited-deportation-policy/). MPI states the expansion was "clear[ed]... to take effect" on 17 July 2026, after a 23 June 2026 panel ruling (CONFIRMED text, https://www.migrationpolicy.org/system/files/2026-10/mpi-deportations-fact-sheet-2026-final.pdf). Status of rehearing or Supreme Court review: UNVERIFIED.
4. Regular proceedings: the person gets an NTA, a master calendar hearing, may apply for asylum, withholding, CAT, cancellation, etc. Asylum must generally be filed within one year of arrival (CRS). Ordered removed, they can appeal to the BIA and then a federal court of appeals.
5. Reinstatement: someone who re-enters after removal can be removed under the old order without a new hearing, and is barred from most relief except fear-based protection (withholding, CAT) (LIKELY, statutory INA 241(a)(5); not re-read this session).

### 2.3 Detention, release, bond, alternatives
- ICE detention peaked at 70,766 in late January 2026, highest in TRAC's series since 2019; 74.2% (52,504) of those had no criminal conviction (a category that includes pending charges). As of 11 July 2026: 65,765 detained, 70.6% (46,436) with no conviction (LIKELY, TRAC via search results, https://tracreports.org/whatsnew/email.260217.html and https://tracreports.org/tracatwork/detail/A6540.html; the pages themselves were not read).

### 2.6 Asylum work permits
- By statute no work permit until 180 days after a complete asylum filing; regulation lets the applicant apply after 150 days (CONFIRMED, CRS R48078). Maximum EAD validity for asylum applicants 18 months as of December 2025 (same source). Applicants who enter without inspection and are not in expedited removal generally file defensively or affirmatively; USCIS counts "applicant-caused delay" against the clock (LIKELY, https://www.uscis.gov/sites/default/files/document/notices/Applicant-Caused-Delays-in-Adjudications-of-Asylum-Applications-and-Impact-on-Employment-Authorization.pdf).
- Change history: the 2020 Trump-era rules that lengthened the wait to 365 days were enjoined and never took effect (LIKELY, background; not re-verified this session). 23 Feb 2026 NPRM, 91 Fed. Reg. 8616: would lengthen the wait to 365 days, pause new initial (c)(8) applications when affirmative processing exceeds 180 days, and end EAD sooner after denials. Comment period closed April 2026. No final rule seen in my sources (UNVERIFIED as of 8 Nov 2026). https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/02/23/2026-03595/employment-authorization-reform-for-asylum-applicants
- Also reported: USCIS paused asylum decisions in December 2025 and partly lifted it in March 2026; a Rhode Island federal court vacated four USCIS pause policies on 5 June 2026; an appeals court let the government continue one policy on 14 August 2026 (LIKELY, law-firm blogs with conflicting dates: https://lawofficeimmigration.com/blog/uscis-asylum-freeze-lifted-2026.html ; https://www.mcenteelaw.com/blog/update-uscis-asylum-hold-new-visa-processing-suspension). Treat as UNVERIFIED in detail.
- One Big Beautiful Bill Act (July 2025) created new fees, including an annual asylum fee (reported $100) and EAD fees; I could not verify the text this session (UNVERIFIED; check Federal Register / USCIS fee notices).

## 3. POLICY TIMELINE 2015 to 2026 (what changed a crosser's options)
Legal status is described as of the search date. Items marked LIKELY are from my background knowledge plus the cited secondary source; dates should be re-checked in the Federal Register before publishing in-game.

- 2015-2016: Large-scale family and unaccompanied child arrivals continue; family detention expanded (2014-15); USBP SW apprehensions 331,333 (FY2015) and 408,870 (FY2016) (CONFIRMED, table in 1.2). Flores settlement governs minors' custody (LIKELY).
- 2017: Executive orders of 25 Jan 2017 prioritise enforcement; USBP SW apprehensions fall to 303,916 (CONFIRMED). CBP creates a Missing Alien Program in 2017 for rescue/prevention (CONFIRMED, CBP mortality page https://www.cbp.gov/newsroom/stats/border-rescues-and-mortality-data).
- 2018: "Zero tolerance": from April 2018 all adults crossing between ports referred for criminal prosecution (8 U.S.C. 1325), leading to family separations; ended by executive order 20 June 2018; court orders (Ms. L. v. ICE) required reunification (LIKELY, background). Attorney General decisions narrowed asylum for domestic and gang violence claims (Matter of A-B-) (LIKELY).
- 2019: Migrant Protection Protocols (MPP, "Remain in Mexico") begins January 2019; transit-country asylum ban (July 2019, litigated); Asylum Cooperative Agreements with Guatemala, Honduras and El Salvador (late 2019); USBP SW apprehensions peak at 851,508 (CONFIRMED) before falling (LIKELY for policy dates; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immigration_policy_of_the_Biden_administration only for later context).
- 2020: Title 42 public-health expulsions begin 21 March 2020 and continue to 11 May 2023 (CONFIRMED date range, CBP page). Several asylum restrictions finalised late 2020 and mostly enjoined (LIKELY).
- 2021: Biden administration pauses MPP (Jan 2021); Title 42 kept for adults and then for single adults and many families; unaccompanied children exempted; USBP SW apprehensions 618,986 and Title 42 expulsions 1,071,075 (CONFIRMED). Court orders compel MPP restart in December 2021 (Texas v. Biden) (LIKELY).
- 2022: MPP restarted Dec 2021; ended by memo in 2022 after Biden v. Texas (Supreme Court, 30 June 2022) (LIKELY). Venezuelan parole program Oct 2022 (LIKELY). USBP SW 1,152,352 Title 8 and 1,103,966 Title 42 (CONFIRMED).
- 2023: January: CBP One app appointments and CHNV parole programs (LIKELY); 11 May: Title 42 ends (CONFIRMED date) and the "Circumvention of Lawful Pathways" rule takes effect, presuming asylum ineligibility for many who enter without a lawful pathway or appointment; a district court vacated it in July 2023 but it stayed in force on appeal (LIKELY: https://asylumist.com/2023/08/02/federal-court-strikes-down-biden-border-policy-delighting-immigration-advocates-and-trump-campaign-officials/). Expedited-removal credible-fear screenings reach 124,780 (CRS count).
- 2024: 4 June: presidential proclamation "Securing the Border" and a joint DHS/DOJ interim final rule that, once daily encounters exceeded a threshold, restricted asylum for people crossing between ports; finalised 7 October 2024 (LIKELY: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Proclamation_on_Securing_the_Border ; the number 10773 for this proclamation is UNVERIFIED). Monthly encounters fell sharply from mid-2024 (CONFIRMED, FY2024 monthly: 251,178 in Dec 2023 vs 55,993 in Sep 2024, CBP FY2024 page). Dec 2024 rule on mandatory bars in fear screenings (Federal Register link in 2.2).
- 2025: 20 Jan: Proclamation 10888 "Guaranteeing the States Protection Against Invasion" suspends entry and asylum access at the southern border for people it covers; CBP One appointment pathway terminated and CHNV parole ended in the weeks after (LIKELY: https://immpolicytracking.org/policies/potus-proclamation-on-guaranteeing-the-states-protection-against-invasion/). 29 Jan: Laken Riley Act expands mandatory immigration detention for people charged with certain offenses (LIKELY, background). Expedited removal extended to the interior for people present under 2 years (CONFIRMED, CRS). Judge Moss (D.D.C.) ruled against the proclamation in July 2025 in RAICES v. Noem (LIKELY, https://cgrs.uclawsf.edu/en/our-work/litigation/raices-v-noem). May 2025: Las Americas v. DHS: district court vacated parts of the Securing the Border rule (LIKELY, https://cgrs.uclawsf.edu/our-work/litigation/las-americas-immigrant-advocacy-center-v-dhs ). Sep-Nov 2025: Yajure Hurtado no-bond ruling; Dec 2025 USCIS asylum hold (see 2.3 and 2.6). July 2025: One Big Beautiful Bill Act enacts immigration fees and enforcement funding (UNVERIFIED details).
- 2026: Feb: Fifth Circuit upholds mandatory detention reading; 23 Feb: DHS EAD NPRM; 18 Feb: C.D. Cal. vacates Yajure Hurtado (stayed 31 Mar by 9th Cir.); 24 Apr: D.C. Circuit affirms in RAICES v. Noem (Mullin) that the proclamation cannot override the asylum statute (divided panel); government reportedly sought certiorari on 24 Aug 2026 (UNVERIFIED current posture) (LIKELY: https://www.npr.org/2026/04/24/g-s1-118707/trump-asylum-ban-us-mexico-border-illegal ; https://raicestexas.org/live-analysis/212f). 23 Jun: D.C. Circuit clears expedited-removal expansion; effective 17 Jul (CONFIRMED text, MPI). 5 Jun: Rhode Island court vacates USCIS pause policies. Border encounters stay near historic lows: FY2026 USBP SW 98,705 (CONFIRMED).

Read-across for design: from 2018 on, "which month and year" changes the legal consequences of a crossing more than nationality does. Show the year-specific rule on the event card and state that rulings were often stayed, reversed or in litigation.

- Northern border: a family of four Indian nationals died of exposure in January 2022 (LIKELY, https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2022/1/28/india-family-froze-to-death-canada-us-border-identified).

### 4.2 Exploitation, debt, violence
- Debt bondage, kidnapping for ransom, extortion and sexual violence along transit routes are documented by IOM, UN agencies, Doctors Without Borders and human-rights groups, but I did not open any of those reports this session (UNVERIFIED as to current rates). The labor exploitation of unauthorized workers (wage theft, retaliation threats, trafficking) is summarised with sources in `country-mexico-addons.md` section 2.2 (NELP 2009: 37% of undocumented workers had a minimum-wage violation in the prior week vs 24% of work-authorized immigrants; SPLC 2009: 41% reported wage theft; LIKELY, both dated) and `country-india-addons.md` 2.4 and `country-nigeria-addons.md` 2.4.
- Legal protections that do not depend on status: wage laws apply to work performed regardless of status; US Supreme Court Hoffman Plastic (2002) limits NLRB back pay for unauthorised workers (LIKELY, in the Mexico add-ons file).

### 4.3 Smuggling cost, aggregate only
- MPI 2021: migrants who used a smuggler spent an average of $7,500; smuggler revenue $2 billion to $6 billion a year (LIKELY, snippet; MPI report not opened).
- CBP statements to Newsweek (about 2021): roughly a hundred to a few thousand dollars for Mexican nationals, $8,000 to $10,000 for Central Americans, $15,000+ for Brazil or Ecuador (LIKELY, stale, https://www.newsweek.com/human-smugglers-charging-15000-per-person-us-border-crossing-1597043).
- India: CBP said in 2018 that Indians paid $25,000 to $50,000 per person (LIKELY, stale, press report). Recent press claims of $50,000 to $100,000 (https://www.washingtontimes.com/news/2024/jun/25/migrants-paying-record-high-50000-to-100000-to-be-/) are not backed by a primary dataset (UNVERIFIED).
- Nigeria: nothing found (GAP).

### 5.2 What is generally closed to someone who entered without inspection (LIKELY, statutory background; not re-read this session)
- Adjustment of status under INA 245(a) requires inspection and admission or parole. A person who crossed without inspection generally cannot adjust inside the US even if a US-citizen spouse or child petitions. Section 245(i) is grandfathered only for petitions or labor certifications filed on or before 30 April 2001.
- Leaving the US to consular process can trigger the 3-year (180+ days of unlawful presence) or 10-year (1+ year) bars, which need a waiver, and which require hardship to a qualifying relative. A permanent bar applies to someone who re-enters unlawfully after accruing more than a year of unlawful presence or after removal.
- DACA is closed to new applicants from my background knowledge (court rulings since 2021); status as of November 2026 UNVERIFIED.
- Federal public benefits: nearly all closed; emergency Medicaid, school enrollment (Plyler v. Doe) and some state programs exist. State driver's licenses and in-state tuition vary by state. ITINs allow tax filing (LIKELY; see Mexico add-ons for sources).
- Work: no federal work authorization unless pending asylum with a granted EAD, a protection status, or another category. Employers must verify work authorization; E-Verify mandates vary by state. Unauthorized employment is unlawful for the worker and employer (see the Mexico/India/Nigeria add-ons, sections on employer sanctions).

### 5.3 Where unauthorized workers tend to work (sources in the country add-ons; all-nationality data)
- Pew (via `country-mexico-addons.md` 2.1): about 8.3 million unauthorized in the labor force in 2022 (about 4.8% of workers); about 1 in 5 household workers and landscapers; 16% of crop workers and meat processors; 13% of construction (LIKELY).
- CMS (via the same file, LIKELY): construction about 20%, accommodation and food 12%, manufacturing 11%, administrative and support 10%, retail 8% of undocumented workers.
- Injury: foreign-born Hispanic workers had 842 work deaths in 2024 (BLS, CONFIRMED in that file), construction the largest sector.
- Indian and Nigerian job lists are in `country-india-addons.md` 2.2 and `country-nigeria-addons.md` 2.3 (informal sectors such as convenience stores and motels, trucking, home health, rideshare; each with caveats there).
- Do not extrapolate: sector data is for all unauthorized workers and all-Latino or all-immigrant groups.

### 5.4 Families and mixed-status households (CONFIRMED, Pew Aug 2025 page above)
- About 4.6 million US-born children under 18 lived with an unauthorized parent in 2023 (4.0 million in 2021; peak 4.9 million in 2016), about 75% of minor children with an unauthorized parent; about 1.5 million minor children were themselves unauthorized (nearly double 2021); about 1.4 million US-born adults lived with an unauthorized parent; about 300,000 babies were born to unauthorized immigrants in 2023.
- US-citizen children cannot petition for parents until age 21, and a parent's unlawful entry usually bars adjustment (5.2). Parents can face detention and removal while children stay in the US; MPI states interior arrests rose to 19,000 deportations a month in May-July 2026 (CONFIRMED, MPI). Child-care arrangements, school attendance and benefit-chilling effects are well documented in research I did not open (UNVERIFIED here).

## 6. COUNTRY NOTES

### Mexico
- Largest historic group but declining share: Mexicans 30% of the unauthorized population in 2023, the smallest on record, 4.3 million (CONFIRMED, Pew). Mexico-born US population about 11.1 million in 2024, a decline since 2010 (11.71 million) and the first fall since 1980 (LIKELY, https://mexiconewsdaily.com/news/mexico-born-population-of-us-drops-to-11-1-million/ ); 22% of all US immigrants; 34% naturalised (LIKELY, MPI via summary).
- Border data pattern (CONFIRMED, 1.3): Mexican apprehensions were about half of USBP SW in FY2020; during Title 42 most Mexicans were expelled (561,329 in FY2021; 668,891 in FY2022); after May 2023 they rose to 502,279 in FY2024 (33% of the USBP SW total) and then fell to 112,077 in FY2025; in FY2026 Mexicans were 68% of a much smaller total (67,011 of 98,705).
- Asylum: low grant ratio (16% FY2024; 751 grants vs 3,910 denials, plus 12,775 not adjudicated) (CONFIRMED).
- Return is common: Mexicans are the largest nationality among removals (about 122,000 in FY2025, UNVERIFIED single source).
- Context for design: much Mexican unauthorized presence is long-settled (more than 10 years), which makes cancellation of removal a live question (5.1).

### India
- Pew 680,000 unauthorized Indians in 2023 (CONFIRMED, third largest after Mexico, Guatemala/El Salvador at 850,000 each, Honduras 775,000: LIKELY, Business Standard snippet); other estimates 220,000 (DHS 2022) and 375,000 (MPI 2022) disagree (see `country-india.md` section 1.6 and `country-india-addons.md` 2.1; method differences).
- Border-crossing increase (CONFIRMED, 1.4): USBP SW Indians 1,089 (FY2020) to 41,715 (FY2023); northern USBP 14,197 in FY2024; total all-CBP Indian encounters 90,415 in FY2024, then 34,146 in FY2025; FY2026 between-port counts about 830. Press reports said Indians were roughly 22% of northern-border encounters in 2024 (LIKELY, https://www.business-standard.com/finance/personal-finance/43-764-indian-migrants-caught-at-us-canada-border-what-s-driving-the-rise-124120200460_1.html ).
- Who is involved: the CBP data public file has no age, gender or home-state breakdown for Indians. Press says mostly single adults (31,480 of 34,146 in FY2025, LIKELY, https://www.vibesofindia.com/?p=368550) and some families and minors; analysts cite economic motives, visa delays and fears among some minority communities (LIKELY, Niskanen and SAIS summaries, https://sais.jhu.edu/sites/default/files/unauthorized-indians.pdf). Home-state claims are UNVERIFIED and not included on purpose.
- Asylum: grant ratio 65% in FY2024 in court (2,364 vs 1,249; CONFIRMED) and about 70% in cases that began with a credible fear claim. The same file shows 2,315 not adjudicated.
- Most Indian unauthorized presence is likely still overstay-based (UNVERIFIED; no split found, `country-india-addons.md` 2.1 says NOT FOUND).

### Nigeria
- Rare, and not visible in CBP's public series (1.5). Pew does not report Nigeria; a viral "3.7 million unauthorized Nigerians" claim was rated false (Dubawa, in `country-nigeria-addons.md`). Overstay is the main form of unauthorized status in the data available (overstay rates for B visas 5.56% in FY2024, F/M/J 11.90%; CONFIRMED in that file).
- Court asylum: 383 grants vs 265 denials in FY2024 (59%; CONFIRMED), small numbers (about 670 decisions).
- Enforcement: about 138 removals in FY2024 (LIKELY). Recent Nigeria-specific travel restrictions are catalogued in `country-nigeria-addons.md` 1.4 and affect legal routes more than border data.

## 7. FIFTEEN CHALLENGE AND EVENT IDEAS
Each is built from a real statistic or rule. Pass and fail states are consequences, not "game over" shaming. No event shows how to cross or avoid law enforcement.

1. **The information gap.** Rule: expedited removal applies to people under 2 years in the US from 2025, effective after the July 2026 ruling. Stat: 69% of 980,000 deportations (Jan 2025 to Jul 2026) were people stopped at the border. Pass: the player reads the notice and asks for a credible fear interview in time. Fail: the character signs forms not understood and is removed in days.
2. **Credible fear interview.** Stat: positive 55% to 42% in FY2024 depending on method. Pass: well-prepared, consistent account, screened in, moves to full hearing. Fail: negative result, a judge reviews within 7 days, character is removed or enters a second review.
3. **Detention without bond.** Rule: Yajure Hurtado and split circuits. Stat: 70.6% of 65,765 detained had no conviction (July 2026). Pass: a lawyer files habeas or a bond request in a favourable circuit and the character is released. Fail: months in detention, lost job, family debt rising.
4. **The hearing you never heard about.** Stat: in absentia orders 311,130 in FY2025; about 39% of completions. Pass: character keeps address current and checks case status. Fail: missed notice produces an in absentia order and a later arrest.
5. **Backlog roulette.** Stat: 3.30 million pending (FY2026); 3.92 million peak. Pass: character plans for a multi-year wait, finances and mental health. Fail: asylum clock slips, one-year filing deadline missed, a key exception not raised.
6. **The work-permit wait.** Rule: apply at 150 days, grant at 180 earliest; Feb 2026 proposal for 365. Pass: character budgets for months without legal work, uses legal aid and savings. Fail: character takes unauthorized work, faces wage theft and employer retaliation, and risks a later credibility or discretion problem.
7. **Debt carried in.** Stat: MPI average $7,500 for those who used a smuggler (stale); Indian-specific figures unverified. Pass: character negotiates a realistic repayment plan with family and a legitimate lender; anxiety stays manageable. Fail: debt spirals, forcing long hours in unsafe work. Show the range and the source weakness.
8. **Exploitation and the way out.** Rule: T and U visas; wage laws apply regardless of status. Stat: U visa cap 10,000 with a backlog over 20 years; 2025 cap met 9 Sept. Pass: character reports a crime or trafficking to a legal aid group, receives certification and a waitlist bona fide determination (work permit, deferred action). Fail: fear of retaliation keeps the character silent and conditions worsen.
9. **Credibility and country evidence.** Stat: Mexico 16% vs India 65% vs Nigeria 59% court grant ratio, FY2024. Pass: the character's well-documented claim succeeds although the national average is low. Fail: the average is not a protection and a thin file loses even with a high national rate. Show that nationality is not the reason.
10. **One year to file.** Rule: asylum filing deadline of one year with exceptions. Pass: character files on time or proves a changed circumstance. Fail: deadline missed, only withholding or CAT remain (a higher bar, no green card path).
11. **Ten-year clock.** Rule: cancellation of removal needs 10 years continuous presence, good moral character, exceptional hardship to a US-citizen or LPR relative; cap 4,000 a year. Pass: after year 10 a lawyer files and wins. Fail: a minor offence, an absence over 90 days, or the hardship standard (very high) defeats the case.
12. **Mixed-status household.** Stat: 4.6 million US-born children live with an unauthorized parent. Pass: the family builds a safety plan (guardian documents, school, emergency contacts) and finds free legal help. Fail: a parent is detained and a child's school and health care are disrupted.
13. **The dead end that is not a dead end.** Rule: no adjustment under 245(a) for most EWI entrants; consular processing triggers the 3/10-year bars; waivers need hardship to a qualifying relative. Pass: the character gets accurate advice early and avoids a wrong move. Fail: the character files a form hoping for a marriage-based green card and triggers removal exposure. Use as a "ask a licensed attorney" event, with no strategy details.
14. **Risk is real, even when everything is done "right".** Stat: 704 Border Patrol-recorded deaths in FY2023 (GAO); 895 or 856 in FY2022 (conflicting); IOM 523 in 2024; all undercounts. Pass/fail is statistical: a risk roll with no player skill that shows loss and its effect on the family. Handle with a content note; never a puzzle with routes.
15. **The policy year.** Rule set differs by year (zero tolerance 2018, MPP 2019, Title 42 2020-23, lawful-pathways presumption 2023, border-closure rules 2024-25, expedited removal 2025-26). Stat: FY2025 USBP SW 237,538 vs FY2023 1,496,067. Pass: the character reads the actual rule for the chosen year and a legal-aid worker helps adapt. Fail: relies on a rumour about a rule that a court has since stayed or reversed.

Optional variants: (a) a "choose how to tell your story to your family at home" event; (b) a "return voluntarily and rebuild" ending, supported by MPI's observation that returns now form a growing share of deportations; (c) a "community clinic" event funded by player savings.
